Research & Recommendations

Can a bot get you Glastonbury?

Short answer: no — and for this festival specifically, trying actively lowers your odds. Here's how the sale really works, and what genuinely raises your chances.

Prepared 15 Aug 2026 2026 is a fallow year Next sale ~Nov 2026 (2027 festival)

The six things that matter

1

Glastonbury is the one big festival where a purchase bot is close to useless. Since 2024 the sale runs on a random queue: everyone on the page at kick-off gets a random place. Speed, refreshing and extra devices don't move you up — they can get your IP flagged and removed.

2

You can't resell a Glastonbury ticket, so the whole economic reason bots exist doesn't apply. Tickets are non-transferable, carry the holder's photo, and are ID-checked at the gate.

3

A bot that automates the real checkout is against See Tickets' terms and probably a criminal offence under the Digital Economy Act 2017 (unlimited fine in England & Wales).

4

"People use AI to guarantee tickets" is mostly myth or marketing. Guaranteed-ticket services are largely scams; the real bot scene targets resale-heavy sites, where anti-bot vendors now block ~98% of bot traffic.

5

What actually works is legitimate and boring: everyone registered, a group of up to six, one fast wired device each, payment ready, on the page early — and don't refresh.

6

The right thing to build and test is your own execution — a local rehearsal simulator that drills the queue → form → payment flow, never touching See Tickets.

01 — Mechanism

How the sale actually works

Understanding the mechanism is the whole game, because it's unlike a normal Ticketmaster drop.

Registration is the real gate

You must register in advance at glastonburyregistration.co.uk with contact details and a passport-style photo. Every person who wants a ticket (age 13+) needs their own registration number before the sale. Un-registered people simply can't buy. Older registrations (pre-2020) are periodically asked to refresh their photo — check yours is valid well ahead.

The sale

Tickets are sold only at glastonbury.seetickets.com. Anywhere else is a scam. Sales come in waves: coach + ticket packages first (a Thursday), then general admission (the following Sunday) — for 2027, expect this around November 2026. One person can buy up to six tickets, entering each attendee's registration number + surname plus a deposit (~£75/ticket); the balance is due in spring.

The random queue — the decisive change

If you're on the page when it opens, you're placed randomly in the queue. Arrive after kick-off and you go to the back. Refreshing can lose your place and look like bot behaviour; multiple tabs or devices can trigger anti-bot software and get your IP blocked. The sale is now a lottery among everyone present at the snapshot, not a race — which is exactly what defeats refresh-bots.

Anti-touting — why reselling is a dead end

Tickets are non-transferable and show the holder's photo; only that person is admitted. Private resale is prohibited and gets the order cancelled. Genuine spares are resold at face value through See Tickets in official resale windows, which sell out in ~30 minutes too.

How fast does it sell out?

Fast, but not milliseconds — which matters for what "automation" could even buy you:

02 — The "AI guarantee" claim

What's real and what isn't

Nobody can guarantee a Glastonbury ticket, and anyone selling that is selling a scam.

  • Guaranteed-ticket / "AI bot" services for sale — treat as fraud. There is no product that guarantees a photo-locked, registration-gated, random-queue ticket. At best they take your money; at worst, your credentials.
  • The genuine ticket-bot scene targets high-resale-value, first-come inventory (the Ticketmaster world). Glastonbury has no resale value, so the incentive that funds serious bots isn't there.
  • The anti-bot industry has caught up. Platforms use Queue-it, Akamai and Cloudflare. Public figures: ~98% of traffic mitigated on a hype event, >50% of blocked bots share one IP, and genuine fans ~50× more likely to get in once mitigation is on.
  • What "AI" realistically does is narrow: solving or bypassing CAPTCHAs, rotating fingerprints/IPs, reading page state. Against a random queue + photo lock, none of that produces a ticket — it just raises your ban risk.

Real-world attempts (now largely obsolete)

Open-source tools like glasto-helper (a Puppeteer refresh app) worked in the old refresh-based era: ~15 browsers reloading the holding page just under the rate limit, jumping to whichever tab reached the ticket page. The 2024 move to a random queue removes the thing they optimised — the technique that used to work is now the technique the system is designed to punish.

03 — Legal & terms

Read this before writing any code

Not legal advice, but the landscape is clear enough to plan around:

  • Digital Economy Act 2017 (s.106) + Breaching Limits on Ticket Sales Regulations 2018. It's a criminal offence to use software to obtain tickets in excess of a seller's limit. Penalty: unlimited fine (England & Wales); up to £50,000 (Scotland).
  • See Tickets' terms prohibit automated access, bots, and circumventing access controls. Breach → order cancelled, account/IP blocked.
  • Computer Misuse Act 1990. Deliberately circumventing technical access controls can raise unauthorised-access questions.
  • What you'll hit first: the anti-bot layer flags you, your IP is blocked, and you're out of this sale — the worst outcome for a fan.

Where the line sits

✓Pre-registering everyone; preparing payment; being ready to type fast — encouraged by the Festival.
✓Practising the checkout flow on a local mock you control.
✓A shared checklist / countdown / "who covers which registration numbers" plan.
✕A script that auto-refreshes or holds a queue slot on the live site — against ToS, counter-productive, ban risk.
✕A script that auto-fills or auto-submits the real purchase or payment — likely a DEA 2017 offence.
✕Multiple devices / IPs / fingerprints for more queue entries — anti-bot trigger and IP ban.

04 — Recommendation

"Simulated testing" done the right way

You spotted the core problem: there's no test period, so you can't prove a concept against the real sale. The professional answer to "I can't test against production" is build a faithful simulation and test against that. For Glastonbury, the thing worth optimising is your group's human execution — so that's what the simulation should exercise.

What ships with this report

An offline rehearsal simulator — a single self-contained HTML file that reproduces the shape of the sale: a random-queue screen (practise waiting without refreshing), a booking form needing six registration numbers + exact surnames with the same validation traps, and a payment screen with a deliberate lockout mirroring See Tickets' ~10-minute freeze on fumbled card details. A stopwatch and error counter score each run. Zero network calls — it's a flight simulator, not an autopilot.

A group readiness pack

  • A shared sheet with every member's registration number + exact surname, pre-verified, assigned so each buyer knows their six.
  • Payment details prepared and correct (UK debit / Visa / Mastercard; know the international-card rules).
  • A connection plan: wired ethernet or strong 5G, on the page a few minutes early, no other heavy downloads.

How to run the drills

  • Individual: complete a booking in well under a minute, zero validation errors.
  • Group: everyone tries; whoever gets in reads registration numbers aloud while another checks — rehearse the hand-off.
  • Failure injection: deliberately fumble a card to feel the lockout; practise recovering calmly.
  • Score and iterate: track time-to-complete and errors. That's your KPI — not requests per second.

05 — The plan

The realistic play for 2027

  • Now → autumn 2026: make sure everyone in your group of up to six is registered with a current photo. This is the single biggest determinant of success.
  • When dates drop (autumn 2026): diary both the coach/package sale (first) and general admission (second). Coach is a genuine second bite.
  • Rehearse until form-filling is muscle memory.
  • On the day: everyone on one fast device each, on the page early, payment ready — then do not refresh. A full group of six multiplies your collective odds.
  • If you miss out: the official See Tickets resale (spring) at face value is your legitimate second chance.
Bottom line. Glastonbury deliberately engineered the sale so preparation and luck decide it, not code. The best "automation" you can build is a group that executes flawlessly — and that you can test.
